# Case File #002 — The Change Healthcare Ransomware Attack

## 1. Executive Summary

## 2. Organization Background
Change Healthcare is a healthcare technology and services organization within UnitedHealth Group. Its infrastructure supports important healthcare administrative and financial functions, including claims processing, payment processing, pharmacy services, and other transactions connecting healthcare providers and payers.

Because of its role in the healthcare ecosystem, disruption to its systems had consequences beyond the organization itself. The 2024 cyberattack affected healthcare providers and operations across the United States, creating difficulties with claims, payments, pharmacy services, and other healthcare processes.

The incident demonstrated how the compromise of a major healthcare technology provider can create widespread operational, financial, and patient-care consequences across the healthcare ecosystem.
## 3. What Happened
On Februrary 21, 2024. Change Healthcare experienced a major cybersecurity attack. According to testimony from UnitedHealth Group CEO Andrew Witty, a cybercriminal group identifying itself as ALPHV or Black cat deployed ransomeware inside change Healthcare's information technology enviroment 

After discovering the attack, Change Healthcare disconnected its systems to contain the incident and prevent further unauthorized access and disruption.

The attack severely disrupted Change Healthcare's claims and payment infrastructure. Healthcare providers experienced difficulties submitting claims, recieving payments, verifying insurances coverage, processing prescriptions, and carrying out other essential administration functions.

The incident developed into a major healthcare-sector crisis because Change attack served as a critical connection point between healthcare providers, payers, pharmacies, and other organizations.

## 4. Attack Timeline
### February 21, 2024 — Attack Identified

Change Healthcare identified a major cyberattack involving ransomware. The organization disconnected its systems as part of its containment response.

### Late February 2024 — Widespread Disruption

The disruption affected healthcare claims, payment processing, pharmacy transactions, eligibility functions, and other services.

### March 2024 — Recovery Begins

UnitedHealth Group began restoring affected services and introduced temporary measures to support healthcare providers experiencing financial disruption.

### March 7, 2024 — Pharmacy Services Recovery

UnitedHealth Group reported that 99% of Change Healthcare pharmacy network services had been restored.

### March 15, 2024 — Electronic Payments Restored

Change Healthcare's electronic payment platform was restored.

### March 18, 2024 — Medical Claims Recovery

UnitedHealth Group reported that medical claims preparation software was being released as part of the recovery process.

### April 2024 — Continued Recovery and Data Investigation

UnitedHealth Group reported that its investigation had identified files containing protected health information and personally identifiable information.

### July 2024 — Breach Report

Change Healthcare filed a breach report with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services concerning the ransomware attack and resulting exposure of protected health information.

## 5. Attack Method
The attack involved ransomware deployed by the ALPHV/BlackCat cybercriminal group.

Public testimony indicates that the attackers gained access to Change Healthcare's environment and deployed ransomware that affected numerous systems. Subsequent testimony from UnitedHealth Group stated that the attackers used compromised credentials to gain access to a Citrix portal that did not have multifactor authentication enabled.

The attackers were able to move within the environment and ultimately deploy ransomware across numerous systems.

The incident demonstrates how weaknesses in identity and access management can provide attackers with an initial foothold that can develop into a large-scale operational disruption.

The confirmed facts available publicly should be distinguished from analytical conclusions about the attacker's activities. This case study therefore avoids presenting unverified technical details as established facts.

## 6. Incident Response
### Identification

The attack was identified on February 21, 2024, after malicious activity was detected within the Change Healthcare environment.

### Containment

Change Healthcare disconnected its systems from the wider healthcare ecosystem to contain the incident and prevent further unauthorized access.

### Eradication

The organization began investigating the compromised environment, rebuilding affected systems, and working to remove the threat from its infrastructure.

### Recovery

Recovery was conducted in stages, prioritizing services that affected patient access to healthcare and medications. Pharmacy services, electronic payments, medical claims, and other critical services were progressively restored.

### Stakeholder Support

UnitedHealth Group established financial assistance programs to help healthcare providers affected by the disruption and provided alternative mechanisms for submitting claims and receiving payments.

### Post-Incident Response

The organization continued investigating the incident and the potential exposure of protected health information. HHS's Office for Civil Rights also opened investigations relating to the breach and compliance with HIPAA requirements.

## 7. Business Impact
The Change Healthcare attack produced significant business and operational consequences.

### Financial Impact

Healthcare providers experienced cash-flow problems because claims could not be processed normally and expected payments were delayed.

UnitedHealth Group established financial assistance programs and advanced billions of dollars to affected providers during the recovery period.

### Operational Impact

The disruption affected claims submission, payment processing, insurance eligibility verification, pharmacy transactions, and other healthcare administrative processes.

### Provider Impact

Hospitals, physicians, pharmacies, and other healthcare organizations were forced to use alternative processes while Change Healthcare systems were unavailable.

### Organizational Impact

The incident required extensive system restoration, cybersecurity investigation, provider support, consumer support, and recovery operations.

### Systemic Impact

The attack demonstrated the risk created when a large portion of healthcare operations depends on a single critical technology and transaction infrastructure provider.

## 8. Healthcare Impact
The incident was particularly significant because healthcare services depend heavily on the availability, integrity, and confidentiality of information systems.

Patients and healthcare providers experienced consequences including difficulties with prescription processing, insurance verification, claims submission, payment, and other administrative processes.

The disruption showed that a cybersecurity incident affecting healthcare infrastructure can become more than an information-technology problem. It can affect access to medications, provider finances, continuity of care, and patient trust.

The incident also created privacy concerns because protected health information and personally identifiable information were identified during the investigation.

This makes healthcare cybersecurity a patient-safety and continuity-of-care issue as well as an information-security issue.

## 9. Security Governance Failures
The incident raises several important security-governance questions.

### Identity and Access Management

Privileged and remote access should be protected with strong authentication, particularly multifactor authentication.

### Cybersecurity Risk Management

Critical systems and dependencies should be identified and continuously assessed for cyber risk.

### Business Continuity

Healthcare organizations should maintain tested alternatives for critical functions when primary technology platforms become unavailable.

### Third-Party Risk

Healthcare organizations should understand how dependent they are on external technology providers and what contingency options exist if those providers experience an outage.

### Security Oversight

Cybersecurity should receive appropriate executive and board-level oversight because a major cyber incident can create operational, financial, regulatory, and patient-safety consequences.

### Resilience

Security programs should not focus only on preventing attacks. They must also ensure that healthcare organizations can detect, contain, recover from, and continue operating during major cyber incidents.

## 10. Key Lessons
1. Healthcare cybersecurity is directly connected to patient safety and continuity of care.

2. Strong identity and access management is essential for protecting critical healthcare infrastructure.

3. Multifactor authentication should be implemented for critical remote and privileged access.

4. Healthcare organizations need tested incident-response and business-continuity plans.

5. Critical third-party dependencies must be identified and managed.

6. Backup and recovery capabilities must support rapid restoration of essential services.

7. Cybersecurity risk requires executive and board-level attention.

8. Healthcare organizations should prepare for both data compromise and operational disruption.

9. Security monitoring and incident detection must be capable of identifying abnormal activity quickly.

10. Cyber resilience requires preparation before an incident, not only response after an attack.

## 11. Conclusion
The Change Healthcare ransomware attack demonstrated how a cyberattack against one critical healthcare technology provider can produce widespread consequences across an entire healthcare ecosystem.

The incident affected technology availability, healthcare payments, claims processing, pharmacy services, provider operations, patient access, and information privacy.

From a cybersecurity perspective, the case highlights the importance of strong access controls, incident response, business continuity, third-party risk management, and security governance.

For healthcare organizations, the central lesson is clear: cybersecurity resilience must be treated as part of patient-care and organizational resilience rather than as an isolated IT responsibility.
